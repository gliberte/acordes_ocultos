# She Wrote This Song at 23 Without Understanding Life—and Needed Half a Century to Truly Sing It

**By the Secret Chords Editorial Team**

---

![Joni Mitchell – Both Sides, Now](images/cover.png)

> *"I've looked at life from both sides now / From win and lose and still somehow / It's life's illusions I recall / I really don't know life at all..."*

In March 1967, thirty thousand feet in the air, a twenty-three-year-old Canadian woman held an open copy of *Henderson the Rain King*, the 1959 novel by Saul Bellow. Roberta Joan Anderson—who had recently adopted the stage name **Joni Mitchell** following a brief marriage that was already coming undone—reached a passage that stopped her cold: flying toward Africa, the protagonist gazes down at the clouds from above and marvels at how, as children, we dreamed of walking on them, and now we cross right through the sky above them in jet planes.

Joni closed the book, rested her forehead against the oval windowpane, and watched that white sea stretching beneath the wings. She pulled out her notebook and began writing almost in a single rush three symmetrical verses—about clouds, about love, and about the mystery of living. By the time the plane touched down, she had tucked into her bag one of the lyric summits of the twentieth century: **"Both Sides, Now."**

What made it astonishing was not merely that a young woman born on the prairies of Saskatchewan had written the definitive meditation on maturity and disillusionment before turning twenty-four. What is truly staggering is that Joni herself would feel for decades that her youthful voice was not yet old enough to carry the weight of her own lines—and that fate would put her through more than half a century of loss, reunion, and medical silence before allowing her to sing it as someone who had finally lived every word.

---

## Act I: The Girl in the Polio Ward and the Secret Winter in Toronto (1953–1966)

Those who wondered in the folk clubs of the late 1960s how someone in her early twenties could write with the weary eyes of an elder knew nothing of what that young woman was already carrying.

The first blow had come in the winter of 1953, when at nine years old she contracted polio during a severe Canadian epidemic. Placed in quarantine far from her parents, more than a hundred kilometers from her home in Saskatoon, she overheard doctors warning that she might never walk again. She spent Christmas alone in an iron hospital bed, singing Christmas carols at the top of her lungs despite the nurses' scolding, silently promising herself that her legs would answer her again. She survived and walked once more, though the illness left the fingers of her left hand weakened; years later, that physical limitation would lead her to invent more than fifty open guitar tunings that reshaped the harmony of popular music forever.

![Joni Mitchell writing beside an airplane window in March 1967](images/scene-01.png)

Yet the wound that shattered her innocence for good arrived in February 1965. At twenty-one, living in Toronto and trying to earn a living singing in small non-union coffeehouses, Joni became pregnant by a fellow art-school student from Calgary who left for California, abandoning her alone and penniless. Without enough money to pay for a decent room or feed a child, and dreading the social stigma that fell upon unwed mothers in mid-1960s Canada, she gave birth to a baby girl whom she named **Kelly Dale Anderson**.

She tried to build a way forward by marrying American folk singer Chuck Mitchell a few weeks later and moving to Detroit, but financial instability and the rapid collapse of that marriage pushed her toward the most heartbreaking decision of her youth: placing her daughter up for adoption in late 1965. In secret, she wrote *"Little Green"* to say goodbye to her, keeping her grief under lock and key for thirty years.

When she opened Saul Bellow's book aboard that plane in March 1967, her marriage had ended only a few months earlier, and less than two years had passed since she had signed the adoption papers. Her ID said she was twenty-three, yet she had already watched nearly every certainty of youth crumble before her eyes:

> *"I'd had three years of solid losses,"* she would later recall. *"I was writing from the first real disillusionment of adulthood."*

## Act II: The Crystal Voice and the Embrace Thirty-Two Years in the Making (1967–1997)

Before Joni ever recorded the song in a studio herself, the singer **Judy Collins** heard it over the telephone in the small hours of the morning in 1967 and cut it with a bright harpsichord arrangement for her album *Wildflowers*. Released as a single in October 1968, it climbed to number eight on the US charts and won the **Grammy Award for Best Folk Performance** at the 11th Annual Grammy Awards in **March 1969**, catapulting its young songwriter into the spotlight. Joni, however, was never wholly convinced by the brisk tempo of that version: she felt it dressed up a confession written out in the cold as a gentle fable.

In 1969, at twenty-five, she placed her own acoustic rendition at the close of her second album, *Clouds*. Accompanied solely by her Martin guitar, her high soprano glided through the verses with an almost crystalline delicacy. Frank Sinatra, Bing Crosby, Willie Nelson, and dozens of other vocalists folded it into their repertoires as an instant standard. And yet, as the years went by, Joni acknowledged a quiet paradox: at twenty-five she had intuited the lyrics, but her voice still sounded too unscarred to truly inhabit them.

![Joni Mitchell reuniting with her daughter after thirty-two years](images/scene-04.png)

Life took charge of chiseling away the rest. She moved through the confessional peak of *Blue* (1971), the harmonic freedom of *Court and Spark* and *Hejira*, celebrated romances that ended in farewells, misunderstandings with the record industry, and long stretches spent taking refuge before her painter's canvases.

Then, in the spring of 1997, after the story of that adoption surfaced in the Canadian press, the seemingly impossible happened: her daughter—named **Kilauren Gibb** by her adoptive parents, by then thirty-two years old and the mother of a young son—had spent years searching for her birth mother and discovered who she was. Their reunion closed a circle of more than three decades of unanswered questions. For the first time since 1965, Joni could look backward without hiding half of her own life.

## Act III: Seventy Musicians in London and the Voice That Learned to Weep Without Tears (1999–2000)

In mid-**1999** (during the summer sessions), at the age of fifty-five, Joni Mitchell accepted an invitation from producer Larry Klein and arranger-conductor **Vince Mendoza** to record at London's legendary **AIR Studios**—housed in the former Victorian church of Lyndhurst Hall—a conceptual album tracing the complete arc of a romance, from first illusion to seasoned acceptance. The final stop on the record could only be *"Both Sides, Now."*

When Joni stepped up to the microphone in the center of an orchestra of **ninety musicians**—drawn from members of the London Symphony Orchestra alongside jazz giants **Herbie Hancock** on piano and **Wayne Shorter** on soprano saxophone—her vocal instrument had become something else entirely. Decades of touring, sleepless nights, and cigarettes had lowered her once-crystalline register into a deep, earthy contralto rich with smoky shading.

![Joni Mitchell recording with the orchestra at AIR Studios in London](images/scene-06.png)

What unfolded in that hall left even the seasoned British orchestral players breathless. Reaching the lines *"Oh, but now old friends, they're acting strange / They shake their heads, they say I've changed,"* this was no longer a young woman imagining the future from an airplane seat: it was a seasoned woman who had won and lost, who had given away a daughter and held her again, sustaining every syllable with the quiet composure of someone who no longer needs to defend herself. As Mendoza's strings rose like a slow tide and Wayne Shorter's soprano saxophone conversed with her fading breath, the song seemed at last to have found the body it had been written for more than three decades earlier.

Released in a limited edition in **February 2000** (and in standard release that March)—and immortalized on film three years later in *Love Actually*, when Emma Thompson's character listens to the record alone in her bedroom trying to gather herself together—that recording proved that time had not withered the song: it had completed it.

## Epilogue: Returning From Silence and the Velvet Armchair at Eighty (2015–2024)

It seemed as though the song's journey had reached its destination in London, yet life still held one final test in store. On March 31, 2015, at seventy-one, Joni Mitchell was found unconscious at her home in Los Angeles after suffering a ruptured brain aneurysm.

Just as had happened to her at age nine in that Saskatchewan hospital, she found herself unable to walk again—and this time, unable to speak as well. For years she stayed away from the stage in quiet, stubborn rehabilitation: she relearned how to form words, how to take short steps, and—watching old videos of her own concerts online because no one else knew her intricate open tunings—she taught her fingers all over again where to place each chord along the fretboard.

![Joni Mitchell performing Both Sides, Now at eighty years old](images/scene-08.png)

In July 2022, she stunned the world with a surprise return at the Newport Folk Festival, surrounded by singer-songwriter Brandi Carlile and a circle of musician friends. And on **February 4, 2024**, at **eighty years old**, she took the stage for the first time in her career to perform at the Grammy Awards ceremony.

Seated in a velvet armchair, two long white braids framing her unmistakable cheekbones, wearing a black beret with her hand resting on her cane, Joni closed her eyes as the opening piano chords of *"Both Sides, Now"* rang out. Inside an auditorium accustomed to loud spectacle, you could have heard a pin drop; dozens of artists spanning three generations listened with tears streaming down their faces as that low, fragile, luminous voice pronounced once more: *"I really don't know life at all."*

Fifty-seven years separated the twenty-three-year-old girl by the airplane window from the eighty-year-old woman beneath the spotlights of Los Angeles. Between those two shores lay a childhood epidemic, an empty crib in Toronto, a reunion, an orchestra in London, and the quiet miracle of learning to speak again. Because some songs arrive in youth like a premonition, and wait patiently across an entire lifetime until our own scars grow tall enough to meet their truth.
